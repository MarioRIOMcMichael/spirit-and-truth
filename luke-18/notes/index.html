Two Men Went Up to Pray

Luke 18:9–14

Coffee and the Word at Jim's · Friday, October 16, 2026

Two men walk into the same temple to do the same thing. One leaves right with God and one doesn't. Jesus tells this story to people who are sure of their own goodness, and turns their scoreboard upside down.

Key Takeaways

  • Luke frames the parable before it starts: it is aimed at people who trusted in themselves that they were righteous and looked down on everyone else (v. 9).
  • The story is built in matching pairs. Its center is two prayers side by side, and its edges are two verdicts: who thought they were righteous, and who God declared righteous.
  • The point is not a new rule about how to pray. It is a reversal that runs through all of Jesus' teaching: the one who lifts himself up is brought low, and the one who goes low is lifted up.

The Lead-In: Doing Your Duty Isn't Credit

A few verses earlier, Jesus tells a short story about a servant who works the field all day and then serves his master supper before he eats. No one throws him a party for it. Then Jesus applies it.

Luke 17:10KJV

10So likewise ye, when ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do.

Hold that next to Luke 18. The Pharisee will stand up and read God a list of the things he was supposed to do anyway, as if they were deposits in an account.

Setting the Scene

Who the story is for

Luke 18:9KJV

9And he spake this parable unto certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others.

Luke tells us the audience before the story starts. Trusting in yourself is the root. Looking down on others is the fruit.

Two men, one temple

The Pharisee. The name likely comes from a Hebrew word meaning "separated." Pharisees were devout, respected laymen who worked hard to live out God's law in daily life. Paul and Nicodemus were Pharisees. Jesus' listeners would have assumed this man was the hero.

The tax collector (the "publican" in the KJV). Rome contracted locals to collect taxes, and many padded the bill and kept the difference. Their own people saw them as traitors and thieves. Jesus' listeners would have assumed this man was the villain.

The Literary Design of the Parable

The story is built in matching pairs that mirror each other around a center. The prayers sit in the middle. The edges answer each other: who trusted they were righteous, and who God declared righteous.

Luke 18:9–14
Av. 9The audience: those who trusted they were righteous and despised others
Bv. 10Two men went up into the temple to pray
Cv. 11–12The Pharisee's prayer: stands, compares, lists his record
C'v. 13The tax collector's prayer: far off, eyes down, one request for mercy
B'v. 14aThis man went down to his house justified rather than the other
A'v. 14bThe principle: whoever exalts himself will be humbled; whoever humbles himself will be exalted

"Righteous" (v. 9) and "justified" (v. 14) come from the same Greek root. The parable opens with righteousness claimed and closes with righteousness received.

Two Prayers, Side by Side

Luke 18:10–14KJV

10Two men went up into the temple to pray; the one a Pharisee, and the other a publican. 11The Pharisee stood and prayed thus with himself, God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican. 12I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess. 13And the publican, standing afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast, saying, God be merciful to me a sinner. 14I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other: for every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.

Word Studies

"Be merciful"ἱλάσθητι

hilasthēti · Luke 18:13

This is not the everyday word for "be kind." It comes from the same family as hilastērion, the word used for the mercy seat, the cover of the ark where atonement was made (Hebrews 9:5; compare Romans 3:25). The tax collector isn't asking God to overlook his sin. He is asking God to deal with it the way God provides. In the Greek he also calls himself "the sinner," not just "a sinner."

"Justified"δεδικαιωμένος

dedikaiōmenos · Luke 18:14

A courtroom word: declared in the right. It shares its root with dikaioi, "righteous," in verse 9. The Pharisee's group trusted they were righteous; God's verdict went to the man who made no claim at all.

"Humbleth himself"ταπεινῶν

tapeinōn · Luke 18:14

To make low, to bring down. Jesus uses the same saying in Luke 14:11 at a dinner where guests scramble for the best seats, and Matthew records it again in 23:12. It is one of his most repeated teachings.

Echoes Across Scripture

Jesus didn't invent this pattern. He's drawing on a thread that runs through Israel's scriptures.

Psalm 51:17KJV

17The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise.

Proverbs 3:34KJV

34Surely he scorneth the scorners: but he giveth grace unto the lowly.

James 4:6 and 1 Peter 5:5 both quote this proverb from its Greek translation: "God resisteth the proud, but giveth grace unto the humble."

Matthew 23:11–12KJV

11But he that is greatest among you shall be your servant. 12And whosoever shall exalt himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.

In Matthew 23, the same saying closes Jesus' warning about religious leaders who love the best seats and titles. Humility isn't only a posture in prayer. It shows up as service.

Reflection Questions

  • Everything the Pharisee said about himself was probably true. Why did his prayer still go wrong?
  • Who is the "this publican" you're tempted to measure yourself against at work, at home, or at church?
  • The tax collector asked for one thing. If you prayed one sentence this week, what would it be?
  • Where could humility look like serving someone this week, without anyone noticing?
