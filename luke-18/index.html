  1. Open in prayer · 6:30

    Pray

    Ask God to quiet the noise from the week and open our hearts to what He wants to show us.

  2. Lead-in: Luke 17:10 · 6:35

    ReadReflect
    10So likewise ye, when ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do.Luke 17:10, KJV ↗

    How it's built

    1. v. 7–9 Setup: a servant comes in from the field and still serves his master supper first. No one thanks him for doing his job.
    2. v. 10a The condition: "when ye shall have done all" that was commanded. Jesus assumes full obedience.
    3. v. 10b The confession: "We are unprofitable servants." Even full obedience earns no bonus.
    4. v. 10c The reason: "we have done that which was our duty." Obedience is what a servant owes, not credit he banks.

    Why it leads in: the Pharisee in Luke 18 does the opposite. He lists his duties as if they were credit.

    • When have you wanted credit for something you were supposed to do anyway?

  3. Read the passage out loud · 6:40

    Read
    9And he spake this parable unto certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others: 10Two men went up into the temple to pray; the one a Pharisee, and the other a publican. 11The Pharisee stood and prayed thus with himself, God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican. 12I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess. 13And the publican, standing afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast, saying, God be merciful to me a sinner. 14I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other: for every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.Luke 18:9–14, KJV ↗ · NIV ↗

    How it's built

    1. v. 9 The frame: Luke names the audience before the story starts: people who "trusted in themselves" and "despised others." The parable is aimed at them.
    2. v. 10 The setup: two men, same place (the temple), same act (prayer). Only the heart will differ.
    3. v. 11–12 The Pharisee: he stands and prays "with himself." He compares ("not as other men… or even as this publican") and lists his record (fasting twice a week, tithing everything). He thanks God but never asks Him for anything.
    4. v. 13 The tax collector: he stands far off, won't lift his eyes, beats his chest. One short request: "God be merciful to me a sinner." He compares himself to no one.
    5. v. 14a The verdict: "this man went down to his house justified rather than the other." Jesus reverses what the crowd expected.
    6. v. 14b The principle: "every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted." It turns the story into a rule for everyone.

    Notice the bookends: verse 9 says they "trusted in themselves that they were righteous," and verse 14 says only the humble man went home "justified" (declared righteous). Righteousness is the theme at both ends, but it's received, not claimed.

  4. Who these two men were · 6:45

    Read

    The Pharisee. The name likely comes from a Hebrew word meaning "separated." Pharisees were devout, respected laymen who worked hard to live out God's law in daily life. Paul and Nicodemus were both Pharisees. Jesus' crowd would have seen this man as the good guy.

    The tax collector (the "publican" in the KJV). Rome hired locals to collect taxes, and many padded the bill and kept the extra. Their own people saw them as traitors and cheats.

    So when Jesus says the tax collector went home justified, the room would have been stunned.

  5. Talk it through · 6:50

    Discuss

    Go wherever the conversation goes. If it drifts, come back to one question: who went home justified, and why?

    • Why did the Pharisee's prayer go wrong, when everything he said about himself was probably true?
    • Where do we compare ourselves to "that guy" instead of looking at God?
    • What does the tax collector have that the Pharisee doesn't?
    • What does humility look like on a Tuesday, at work or at home?

  6. Humility looks like serving · 7:10

    ReadDiscuss
    11But he that is greatest among you shall be your servant. 12And whosoever shall exalt himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.Matthew 23:11–12, KJV ↗

    How it's built

    1. Context Matthew 23 is Jesus' public warning about the scribes and Pharisees: they love the best seats and titles (v. 5–10).
    2. v. 11 The principle: the greatest is the servant. Greatness is measured by service, not position.
    3. v. 12 The reversal: the same exalt/abase, humble/exalt saying as Luke 18:14 (and Luke 14:11). Jesus repeated it in different settings.

    Put together: Luke 17 says obedience isn't credit, Luke 18 says comparison isn't righteousness, and Matthew 23 says humility shows up as serving.

    Verse 12 says almost word for word what Jesus says at the end of Luke 18.

    • Who's someone you know that serves without needing anyone to notice?

Teaching it through

Teaching notes in a kingdom-preaching style: plain talk, everyday pictures, and points that stick. Use what helps and leave the rest.

  1. The problem: grading on the wrong curve

    The Pharisee didn't measure himself against God. He measured himself against the man in the back row. That's like a student grading his own test by finding the worst paper in the class and saying, "Well, at least I beat him." Comparing yourself with the worst person in the room will always make you look good. Comparing yourself with a holy God will always send you to your knees.

    Luke 18:9 tells us who the story is for: people who "trusted in themselves." Self-trust is the root. Despising others is the fruit.

  2. The posture: standing tall vs. standing far off

    Picture two men in a doctor's office. One talks the whole time about how healthy he is, with his arm bleeding all over the floor. The other one sits down and says, "Doc, I'm sick. Help me." Which one gets treated? The doctor can only treat the man who admits he needs treatment.

    The Pharisee "stood and prayed thus with himself" (v. 11). The tax collector stood "afar off," eyes down, beating his chest (v. 13). One came to report. The other came to repent.

  3. The plea: mercy, not merit

    The Pharisee's prayer was a résumé: what I don't do, what I do, how often I do it. Count the requests: zero. He didn't come to get anything from God, because he didn't think he needed anything. The tax collector had one sentence: "God be merciful to me a sinner." In the Greek it reads "the sinner," not just "a sinner." He wasn't comparing. He was confessing.

    The word behind "be merciful" is tied to the place of atonement, the mercy seat. He wasn't asking God to overlook his sin. He was asking God to deal with it God's way.

  4. The pronouncement: God has the final word

    "Justified" is a courtroom word. It's the judge's verdict. You can argue your case all day long, but when the gavel comes down, the only opinion that counts is the one on the bench. Both men went home that day. Only one went home with a not-guilty verdict, and it wasn't the one who argued his own case.

    Verse 9 says they trusted they were righteous. Verse 14 says God declared the humble man righteous. You don't claim it. You receive it.

  5. The principle: the kingdom runs upside down

    In the world, you climb up to get ahead. In God's kingdom, you get low to go up. Think about a restaurant: the most important person to your meal isn't the one in the nicest suit. It's the one carrying the plate. Jesus says the greatest in the kingdom is the servant (Matthew 23:11). Exalt yourself and God brings you down. Humble yourself and God lifts you up (Luke 18:14; Matthew 23:12).

    And Luke 17:10 keeps us honest: even when we've done everything right, we've only done our duty. Obedience isn't a bonus. It's the job.

  6. Bring it home

    So here's the question for the week: whose paper are you grading yourself against? At work, at home, at church, the moment you catch yourself saying "at least I'm not like him," stop. Trade the comparison for a confession, and trade the spotlight for a towel. That's the man who goes home justified.
