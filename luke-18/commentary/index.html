Two Men Went Up to Pray

Luke 18:9–14

Listen to the commentary

About 11 minutes. Read along below.

Good morning, and welcome. Grab your coffee and your Bible. This week we're in Luke chapter eighteen, a short story Jesus told about two men who went to the temple to pray. It's only six verses long, but it turns everything upside down.

Before we get there, let's back up one chapter.

The Lead-In

In Luke chapter seventeen, Jesus tells a quick story about a servant. This man works the field all day. He comes in tired and hungry. And instead of sitting down to eat, he puts on his apron and serves his master supper first. Nobody throws him a party for that. It's his job.

Then Jesus says this, in verse ten:

"So likewise ye, when ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do."

Notice what Jesus assumes. He says when you've done all of it. Not some. All. And even then, the right response isn't, "Look what I did." It's, "I only did what I owed."

Hold on to that. Because in the very next chapter, we're going to meet a man who reads God a list of the things he was supposed to do anyway, as if every one of them were a deposit in the bank.

The Passage

Here's Luke chapter eighteen, verses nine through fourteen.

"And he spake this parable unto certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others: Two men went up into the temple to pray; the one a Pharisee, and the other a publican. The Pharisee stood and prayed thus with himself, God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican. I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess. And the publican, standing afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast, saying, God be merciful to me a sinner. I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other: for every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted."

Look at how Luke opens. Before the story even starts, he tells us who it's for. People who "trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others." That's the root and the fruit. Trusting yourself is the root. Looking down on everybody else is what grows out of it.

Who These Two Men Were

Now, to feel the punch of this story, we have to hear it the way the first crowd heard it.

The Pharisee was the good guy. The name likely comes from a Hebrew word meaning "separated." Pharisees were devout, respected men who worked hard to live out God's law every single day. The apostle Paul was a Pharisee. So was Nicodemus. When Jesus said, "a Pharisee went up to pray," everybody listening would have nodded. Of course he did.

The tax collector, the "publican" in the King James, was the bad guy. Rome hired local men to collect taxes from their own people, and a lot of them padded the bill and pocketed the difference. Their neighbors saw them as traitors and thieves.

So when Jesus gets to the end and says the tax collector is the one who went home right with God, that room would have gone quiet. That's not how the story was supposed to end.

Two Prayers

Let's put the two prayers side by side.

The Pharisee stands up and prays, the King James says, "with himself." He's talking to God, but he's really talking about himself. He thanks God that he's not like other men. Then he looks across the room and points. "Or even as this publican." Then he reads his record. I fast twice a week. I give a tenth of everything.

And here's the thing. All of that was probably true. He wasn't lying. But count how many times he asks God for anything. Zero. He didn't come to receive. He came to report.

Now the tax collector. He stands far off. He won't even lift his eyes. He beats on his chest. And he prays one sentence. "God be merciful to me a sinner."

He doesn't compare himself to anybody. He doesn't list anything. He just tells the truth about himself and asks for mercy.

Teaching It Through

So what's Jesus getting at? Let's walk through it.

First, the problem. The Pharisee was grading on the wrong curve. He didn't measure himself against God. He measured himself against the man in the back row. It's like grading your own test by finding the worst paper in the class and saying, "Well, at least I beat him." Compare yourself to the worst person in the room, and you'll always look good. Compare yourself to a holy God, and you'll end up on your knees.

Second, the posture. Picture two men at the doctor's office. One talks the whole time about how healthy he is, with his arm bleeding all over the floor. The other sits down and says, "Doc, I'm sick. Help me." Which one gets treated? The doctor can only help the man who admits he needs help.

Third, the plea. When the tax collector says, "be merciful," that's not the everyday word for "be nice to me." In the Greek, it comes from the same family of words as the mercy seat, the cover of the ark of the covenant, where atonement was made for sin. He isn't asking God to look the other way. He's asking God to deal with his sin the way only God can. And in the Greek, he actually calls himself "the" sinner. Not a sinner. The sinner. He's not comparing anymore. He's confessing.

Fourth, the verdict. Jesus says this man "went down to his house justified." That's a courtroom word. It's what the judge says when the gavel comes down. You can argue your case all day long, but the only opinion that counts is the one from the bench. Both men went home that day. Only one went home with a verdict of "not guilty." And it wasn't the one who argued his own case.

And here's something beautiful in how Luke wrote this. In verse nine, the word "righteous" and in verse fourteen, the word "justified" come from the same root. The story opens with righteousness claimed, and it closes with righteousness received. You don't claim it. You receive it.

Humility Looks Like Serving

Jesus closes with a principle. "Every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted."

He didn't just say that once. He said it at a dinner in Luke chapter fourteen, where the guests were scrambling for the best seats. And Matthew records it again in chapter twenty-three, verses eleven and twelve:

"But he that is greatest among you shall be your servant. And whosoever shall exalt himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted."

In God's kingdom, everything runs upside down. In the world, you climb up to get ahead. In the kingdom, you go low to be lifted up. Think about a restaurant. The most important person to your meal isn't the man in the nicest suit. It's the one carrying the plate.

So put the three passages together. Luke seventeen says obedience isn't credit. Luke eighteen says comparison isn't righteousness. And Matthew twenty-three says humility shows up as serving.

Bring It Home

So here's the question to carry into this week. Whose paper are you grading yourself against?

At work, at home, even at church, the moment you catch yourself thinking, "Well, at least I'm not like him," stop right there. Trade the comparison for a confession. Trade the spotlight for a towel.

And if you only prayed one sentence this week, what would it be? The tax collector's prayer is a pretty good place to start. "God be merciful to me a sinner."

That's the man who goes home justified.
